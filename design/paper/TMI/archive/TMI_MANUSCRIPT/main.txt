Measurement Overlap Governs Disagreement-Based
Reliability in Deterministic MRI Slice Interpolation
Anonymous Authors

Abstract—Deterministic through-plane MRI interpolation can
produce plausible missing slices without indicating when they are
inaccurate. Disagreement between reconstructions is a natural
retrospective reliability signal, but it observes only differences
between their errors and can miss common-mode failures. We test
the hypothesis that measurement overlap controls this blindness.
Two frozen neural interpolators (HAT and SwinIR) reconstructed
identical missing physical planes from either disjoint ×4 anchor
lattices or the same anchors under a maximal-overlap orderreversal control. Dense targets were used only to synthesize
retrospective alternative acquisitions and evaluate error. On 24
fastMRI brain T2 cases, zero-overlap disagreement correlated
more strongly with target error than maximal-overlap disagreement (Spearman difference 0.355; case-clustered 95% CI, 0.225–
0.557). On 22 NingXia knee T2 series from eight patients, the
difference was 0.193 (patient-clustered 95% CI, 0.113–0.336).
Effects remained positive for each architecture, while linear
and cubic interpolators independently produced case-level correlations of 0.958–0.977 under disjoint sampling. Across frozen
analyses, shared consensus error accounted for median 80.9%
and 86.5% of squared error. Auxiliary IXI brain T2 results (115
cases) showed highly architecture-consistent difficulty but did not
enter the controlled overlap test. These findings support a scoped
design principle: reliability contexts should reduce shared errorgenerating measurements rather than merely maximize output
diversity. Low disagreement cannot certify correctness.
Index Terms—Magnetic resonance imaging, slice interpolation,
image reconstruction, reliability, uncertainty estimation, consistency, sampling.

I. I NTRODUCTION
NISOTROPIC magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) volumes often have substantially coarser through-plane than
in-plane resolution. Interpolating unacquired slices can ease
visualization and downstream volumetric processing, and modern slice-synthesis networks improve over classical interpolation in many settings [1], [2]. Yet a deterministic interpolator normally returns one image, even when the acquired
anchors do not constrain the missing anatomy sufficiently.
A deployment-facing reliability signal must therefore identify
likely failure without accessing the dense target that is missing
in practice.
Model ensembles, stochastic sampling, test-time augmentation (TTA), and cross-context consistency estimate reliability
from variation among plausible outputs [3]–[6]. Their common
premise is useful but one-sided: disagreement witnesses that at
least one prediction differs, whereas agreement need not imply
correctness. If two reconstructions inherit the same smoothing,
boundary limitation, or anchor-induced bias, subtracting them
removes the very error one wishes to detect. This issue is especially relevant to interpolation because nearby reconstruction
contexts can reuse the same acquired slices.

We investigate measurement overlap as a control variable
for error observability (Fig. 1). The same missing physical
plane is reconstructed twice at the same nominal factor. In
one condition, the two contexts draw from phase-shifted ×4
anchor lattices with no shared slices. In the maximal-overlap
control, they use the identical anchor lattice but reverse its
through-plane order before mapping the output back. Case,
target, model, plane, evaluation support, and number of measurements remain paired. The control has a known limitation—
reversal changes ordering—so we interpret it as a mechanistic
retrospective intervention, not a complete causal identification
of overlap alone.
The study makes four contributions. First, it formalizes
consensus blindness as cancellation of aligned reconstruction
errors and links the observable fraction of squared error
to context diversity. Second, it conducts a target-preserving
measurement-overlap intervention in brain and knee T2 MRI,
with corrected patient-level inference for the multi-series knee
cohort. Third, it tests architecture, analytical-interpolator, rawdiversity, and stable-wrong boundaries. Fourth, it evaluates
a methodological consequence: whether disjoint-context disagreement retrospectively ranks the benefit of denser sampling.
All claims concern deterministic MRI slice interpolation; we
do not claim calibrated uncertainty, prospective acquisition
benefit, or clinical safety.
II. R ELATED W ORK
A. MRI Slice Synthesis and Interpolation
Classical linear and cubic splines estimate missing samples from smoothness assumptions [7]. Deep slice-synthesis
methods instead learn anatomical priors. SAINT conditions
synthesis on spatial position and supports variable upsampling
factors [1]; I3Net combines inter- and intra-slice information
for medical slice synthesis [2]. These approaches primarily
optimize reconstruction fidelity. Our question is complemen-

B. Reconstruction Uncertainty and Reliability
MRI reconstruction uncertainty has been estimated through
variational latent models, SURE, Bayesian parameter distributions, and posterior sampling [5], [6]. In neuroimage
enhancement, predictive uncertainty has also been evaluated
against errors and distribution shift [8]. These methods address
probabilistic reconstruction or learned uncertainty. We do not
estimate a calibrated posterior; we study why a broad class
of output-disagreement signals can be blind when errors are
shared.
C. Consistency, Diversity, and Shared Error
Deep ensembles and stochastic perturbations use diversity
as evidence of uncertainty [3], [4]. Ensemble theory emphasizes that diversity interacts with, rather than replaces,
individual predictor accuracy [9], [10]. Self-consistency also
has a long history in MRI reconstruction, including measurement consistency in SPIRiT [11]. Our closest distinction is
intervention-level: we manipulate overlap of acquired throughplane measurements for the same target and quantify reliability
ranking, rather than interpreting arbitrary output dispersion as
uncertainty.

IV. M EASUREMENT-OVERLAP H YPOTHESIS
A target-preserving context is a set of acquired anchors
and reconstruction operations that predicts the same physical
missing plane. Measurement overlap is the number of identical
acquired slices shared by the two bracketing contexts. At factor
four, phase-0 anchors are z = 0, 4, 8, . . . and phase-2 anchors
are z = 2, 6, 10, . . .. Targets with z mod 4 ∈ {1, 3} are
missing from both grids, and the two grids share no anchors
(Fig. 2).
The maximal-overlap control uses the phase-0 anchors in
both contexts, reverses the sparse sequence for one run, and
maps its prediction back to physical z. It shares two bracketing
anchors for each interior target. Our prespecified directional
prediction is that greater overlap increases error alignment,
lowers the observable error fraction, and weakens ranking of
target error by disagreement. Only the final ranking comparison was available in the frozen TMI evidence; covariancearrow tests and cleaner flip controls are reserved for the
separated exploratory conference branch.

C. Reliability Scores and Endpoints
For paired predictions a, b, the primary plane score was
mean absolute disagreement on the deployment mask |a| >
0.01 max |a|. The retrospective endpoint was mean absolute
error of the stored phase-0 prediction on that identical mask.
Dense targets entered only phase-grid simulation and evaluation, never the reliability score. Examination or model–series
scores were medians across matched generated planes.
Frozen secondary comparators included: (i) target-excluded
committee disagreement; (ii) Physical Consistency (PC-v1),
the median absolute difference among NIfTI-affine matched
predictions at ×2, ×4, and ×6; (iii) four-view flip-TTA; and
(iv) adjacent-anchor RMSE, computed before reconstruction.
Ranking used Spearman correlation and normalized area under the risk–coverage curve (nAURC). The latter normalizes
empirical selective risk between oracle and reverse-oracle
rankings. Densification benefit was the realized reduction in
reconstruction MAE from stored ×4 to ×2 outputs.
D. Statistical Analysis
Repeated planes, factors, and architectures were not treated
as independent patients. Primary intervals used 5,000 cluster
bootstrap replicates, resampling 24 fastMRI cases or eight
NingXia patient groups with all subordinate observations
retained. The PC–TTA comparison used whole-cluster score
swaps; its historical NingXia 22-series permutation was superseded by a 10,000-swap patient-group analysis. Plane-level
correlations describe ranking across evaluated planes, with
clustered intervals quantifying sensitivity to independent-unit
sampling. Analyses were paired by dataset, case, model, and
plane. No multiple-comparison-adjusted universal superiority
claim is made.

B. Controlled Measurement-Overlap Intervention
Figure 3 and Table II report the primary comparison. On
fastMRI, zero-overlap disagreement had plane-level Spearman
ρ = 0.960 with phase-0 MAE, versus 0.605 for the maximaloverlap reversal control, a difference of 0.355 (case-clustered
95% CI, 0.225–0.557). On NingXia, the corresponding values
were 0.891 and 0.699, a difference of 0.193. Correct resampling of eight patient groups gave a 95% CI of 0.113–0.336.
Endpoint differences were exactly zero because both scores
were evaluated against the same stored phase-0 error.

C. Architecture and Analytical Generalization
The effect was positive for HAT and SwinIR separately in
both cohorts (Table II; Fig. 4a). Under zero-overlap grids,
classical linear and cubic interpolation also produced strong
case-level reliability: Spearman correlations were 0.977 and
0.974 on fastMRI and 0.958 and 0.967 on NingXia (Fig. 4b).
These analytical results show that the useful witness is not
uniquely tied to neural stochasticity. Because the analytical
experiment lacked a frozen matched maximal-overlap comparator, it supports disjoint-context generality rather than the
overlap contrast itself.

D. Raw Diversity Is Not Sufficient
On fastMRI, zero-overlap median raw disagreement was
only 59.1% (HAT) and 77.2% (SwinIR) of maximal-overlap
disagreement, yet its error ranking was substantially stronger.
Thus a condition can produce larger output differences while
being a worse reliability ranker. NingXia showed the opposite
magnitude ordering, so raw scale is cohort dependent. The
conclusion is deliberately narrow: larger raw output diversity
is neither necessary nor sufficient for better ranking in these
controls.
E. Retrospective Predictive Consequence
Disjoint ×4 disagreement predicted the realized ×4 → ×2
reduction in reconstruction MAE with ρ = 0.811 (95% CI,
0.592–0.904) on 48 fastMRI model–case units and ρ = 0.626
on 44 NingXia model–series units; patient-cluster correction
gave 95% CI 0.200–0.844. At a prespecified top-25% retrospective densification budget, score-ranked MAE reductions
were 33.36 versus 21.37 for random selection on fastMRI and
35.39 versus 24.25 on NingXia (Fig. 5b). The fastMRI policy
captured 97.6% of the oracle gain above random; NingXia
captured 76.5%. This is retrospective predictive validity using
already dense targets, not prospective or clinical acquisition
optimization.

VII. D ISCUSSION
The experiments support a mechanistic account of disagreement blindness in deterministic MRI interpolation. Reconstruction contexts can share a large consensus error, and
Eq. (3) shows why aligned errors disappear under subtraction.
The controlled phase-grid intervention changes the acquired
information supporting the same target. Disjoint grids rank
error better than a same-anchor reversal context across two
anatomies and two neural architectures, while analytical interpolation provides an estimator-family replication. The result
is stronger than observing that perturbations produce different
outputs: the paired endpoint is fixed, measurement identity is
recorded, and a raw-magnitude control demonstrates that more
output variation is not itself the operative goal.
The findings also clarify what disagreement can and cannot
mean. High disjoint-context disagreement is useful evidence
of context-sensitive failure. Low disagreement is ambiguous
because both contexts may share bias. In practice, reliability
systems should therefore seek contexts that reduce dependence
in error-generating information, while retaining explicit stablewrong safeguards. This principle complements probabilistic
reconstruction: even posterior or ensemble samples can underestimate risk if their construction does not vary the source
of shared error.
The retrospective densification result offers a methodological consequence. Cases with larger disjoint disagreement
tended to realize larger gains from denser stored sampling.
That relationship is promising for acquisition-allocation research because the score itself uses only alternative sparse contexts. However, constructing two disjoint ×4 grids consumes
the union of measurements equivalent to denser coverage in
this simulation. A practical policy would require a lowercost pilot, sequential sampling, or a pre-inference surrogate
evaluated prospectively. The present study does not solve that
deployment problem.
The IXI evidence adds scale and a second brain cohort to
the broader observation that interpolation difficulty is largely

shared across methods. It does not constitute an IXI replication of the frozen overlap intervention, and we intentionally
keep newer IXI overlap experiments outside this manuscript’s

VIII. L IMITATIONS
All phase grids are retrospective simulations from dense
volumes; no scanner prospectively changed its sampling. The
primary controlled evidence includes one brain T2 cohort and
one small knee T2 cohort; NingXia has only eight independent
patient groups despite 22 series. IXI contributes auxiliary
difficulty evidence, not the main intervention. Only HAT and
SwinIR support the decisive neural phase-grid experiment;
I3Net was infeasible without artificial padding. The maximaloverlap reversal control changes through-plane ordering and
may invoke equivariance effects beyond overlap. A strict
multi-level overlap ladder with fixed two-anchor distances is
geometrically infeasible; cleaner controls remain exploratory.
Intensity-derived MAE and disagreement require comparable preprocessing within each paired profile and do not
support cross-cohort absolute calibration. The study has no
reader task, pathology label, clinical endpoint, prospective
adaptive acquisition, or safety analysis. It cannot identify
which output is wrong, guarantee accuracy from agreement,
or establish universal superiority over uncertainty estimators.
Common-mode blur and boundary failures remain explicit
counterexamples. Finally, parts of the evidence were generated
after inspecting earlier results; except where a protocol was
historically frozen, analyses are labeled exploratory rather than
confirmatory.

X. C ONCLUSION
In deterministic MRI slice interpolation, disagreement observes differences between reconstruction errors, not error
itself. Shared measurements can align errors and conceal

5

them. Across paired brain and knee T2 experiments, targetpreserving disjoint anchor grids ranked retrospective error
more effectively than a maximal-shared-anchor reversal control, and the effect persisted across two neural architectures
and analytical interpolation. The supported principle is narrow:
design reliability contexts to reduce shared error-generating
information. Agreement alone cannot certify correctness.
