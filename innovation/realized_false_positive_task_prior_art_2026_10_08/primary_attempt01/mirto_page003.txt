Validation methodology. Maier-Hein et al. showed that
challenge rankings change with the metric and the ag￾gregation [45], and the Metrics Reloaded consortium
catalogued metric pitfalls and recommendations [46,56],
including the dependence of overlap metrics on struc￾ture size [61]. Multiverse analysis [60] and specification
curves [59] evaluate a conclusion over all defensible anal￾ysis paths; the many-analysts design shows how widely
reasonable paths diverge [58]. Selection on the evaluation
data inflates estimates [62,20]. Across medical imaging,
Varoquaux and Cheplygina traced how biases enter at
every step from data to evaluation [63]; Christodoulou et
al. found that more than half of the MICCAI 2023 seg￾mentation papers did not assess performance variability
and that only one reported confidence intervals [23]; and
a Bayesian analysis of published comparisons estimated a
probability of a false outperformance claim above 5% for
86% of classification and 53% of segmentation papers [22].
In machine learning generally, data sampling, initialisa￾tion and hyperparameter choice add enough variance to
change benchmark conclusions [17]. MIRTO brings these
ideas to UAD and adds what segmentation by threshold￾ing needs: an explicit operating point.
Distribution-free control of operating points. Conformal
risk control (CRC) chooses a threshold on calibration data
so that the expected value of a bounded, monotone loss on
a new exchangeable case is at most α [3]; risk-controlling
prediction sets (RCPS) [8] and Learn-then-Test [2] give
control with high probability over the calibration set in￾stead; see [1] for an introduction. We use the loss “this
scan exceeds its false-positive budget”. For such an in￾dicator loss CRC reduces to split-conformal calibration,
whose coverage conditional on the calibration set follows
a known Beta law [64,1]; we use that law to state how
often a small calibration set misses its target. Covariate￾adjusted ROC analysis [36] provides the tool we use to
separate anatomy from detection in slice-level scores.
1.2 Contributions
Our contribution is an evaluation protocol, the exact ac￾counting that makes what it reports interpretable, and
one method-level improvement evaluated with it; the ex￾periments validate the protocol and apply it.
1. Registration gate and label-free diagnostics.
A gate that accepts correct registrations and rejects
flips, transposes and two-slice offsets, and five dis￾cordance diagnostics computable without extra la￾bels, two of which detect injected in-plane errors with
power 1.000 at a false-alarm rate of 0.05 in 30 subjects
(Section 2.3).
2. Operating points that account for threshold
transfer. Validation-only thresholds, realised rather
than nominal burden, a threshold-transfer gap with
an exact decomposition of any contrast into a map
term and a transfer term (Identity 1), and a conformal
per-scan burden guarantee, for which we apply the
known training-conditional law of split-conformal cal￾ibration to state, from the calibration size alone, how
often a calibration set misses its target (Lemma 1;
Section 2.4).
3. Exact accounting of post-processing and met￾rics. Elementary identities, applied to UAD, that
split a post-processing gain at equal burden into an
effect at fixed threshold and a threshold relaxation,
state exactly when deleting a component raises Dice
(Identities 4 and 5), and decompose pooled Dice and
pooled AUROC so as to explain when subject-level
and pooled rankings disagree (Identities 2 and 3).
Their value lies in what they let a reader attribute,
not in their difficulty.
4. An evaluation multiverse. Ten axes of evaluation
choices, flip shares read together with the size of each
difference, per-metric variance shares with bootstrap
intervals, and the training data as an eleventh axis
(Section 2.7).
5. Latent aggregation for REFLECT. A training￾free change to how REFLECT combines its latent
channels, which raises Dice at equal burden in two
independently trained models (Sections 2.9 and 3.10).
We apply the protocol to four UAD methods and two
model-free baselines. We do not claim that any method is
best, nor that the inference is confirmatory: the 312 test
subjects served both to develop the protocol and to test
its hypotheses, so every test is exploratory (Section 2.8).
