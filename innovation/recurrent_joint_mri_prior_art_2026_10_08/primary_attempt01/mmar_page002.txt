1. Introduction
White matter hyperintensities (WMH) and ischaemic stroke lesions (ISL)
are pathological features often visible on magnetic resonance imaging (MRI)
scans of individuals with cerebral small vessel disease (SVD) (Duering et al.,
2023), a vascular disease affecting the small blood vessels. Their automatic
segmentation and differentiation is inherently challenging due to their sim￾ilar visual appearance in most MRI sequences: both lesion types present
as hypointense on T1-weighted (T1w) MRI sequences and hyperintense on
T2-weighted (T2w) MRI sequences, including the fluid-attenuated inversion
recovery (FLAIR) sequence. Although diffusion-weighted images (DWI) can
sometimes distinguish ISL from WMH, this is typically only effective when
ISL is acute and, as apparent diffusion coefficients vary with age, body
temperature, scanning settings, and tissue specific characteristics, this MRI
modality is not always a reliable way to identify ISL (Edlow et al., 2017).
While visually differentiating these confounding lesions is difficult, it is
a task well suited to deep learning, as neural networks excel at uncovering
highly complex patterns. To achieve robust performance, however, these
models must be trained on as much expertly annotated data as possible, and
this data should ideally originate from a large variety of sources (i.e., imaging
acquisition protocols and MRI scanners). Beyond a general scarcity of such
data, model development is hindered by two main factors: 1) not all datasets
with WMH or ISL annotations have them for both lesion types; and 2) clinical
datasets rarely provide a complete, pristine set of MRI sequences: sequences
are frequently missing, or present but severely degraded by artefacts. The
